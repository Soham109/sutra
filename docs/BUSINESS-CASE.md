# The business case, argued honestly

There are zero registered users of this product outside the team, zero paying customers, zero merchant partners beyond one configured Shopify development store, and no traction data of any kind. Every claim below has to survive that starting condition.

## The one-line case, and its limit

Sutra is infrastructure for one narrow, real moment: N people who must jointly authorize one purchase, before it happens, at a merchant that will accept per-person capped payment credentials instead of a single card. That moment is genuinely unserved by any shipping payment protocol — AP2 v0.2, Stripe ACP, Visa Intelligent Commerce, and Prava's own mandate API are all single-principal by construction (`spec/AP2-EXTENSION.md`, "The gap"). It is also narrower than "splitting a bill." Most of what people mean by that phrase is already served by one person fronting the card or the group settling up afterward, and much of this document is about being precise about where those two behaviors stop being good enough and Sutra's narrower case starts being worth paying for.

## Who has this problem

The GMP/1-shaped problem — joint, pre-purchase, per-person-capped consent bound to one specific merchant checkout — is not the everyday dinner-bill split. That case is low-stakes, high-trust, and already well served. The shape Sutra fits shows up in higher-stakes, lower-frequency purchases: event and ticket blocks bought by a friend group, shared travel bookings, one-off big-ticket purchases among flatmates, club dues and equipment buys, and workplace group purchases where fronting a few hundred dollars is a real ask. No usage data exists to size any of this, because there are no users — the number that would matter (how often a friend group in a given city actually buys a block of tickets or splits a big one-off purchase) is knowable, and ticketing and travel platforms have it internally; it's the first thing a credible next step should go get rather than assume.

## The wedge

Of the five rows in the README's money-boundary table, exactly one completes an actual charge: a merchant with a real Sutra/Prava adapter. Today that row has zero production merchants and one test-only merchant (the configured Shopify development store, which explicitly moves no real money). So the only case a real business can be built on is getting a merchant to accept Prava-mandated group checkout — nobody has done that yet, which makes the wedge a go-to-market bet, not a currently-live product-market fit.

Event and experience ticketing is the strongest candidate, for four reasons. It's a purchase groups already coordinate informally in chat, so the coordination layer Sutra already built maps onto real behavior. Per-person ticket prices ($20–300) are high enough that the processing-fragmentation tax argued below stays a small fraction of the purchase. The merchant side has acute pain today: group ticket sales either don't happen because one person won't front $800 for eight tickets, or they happen with real collection risk. And ticketing sits behind a small number of large platforms, which makes the two-sided cold-start problem tractable — a handful of platform integrations could reach a large number of consumer groups.

Explicitly not the wedge: the restaurant/at-venue case that is the product's own flagship demo. That's exactly where UPI Collect and point-of-sale split tender already work today, and where Sutra's own `at_venue` rail charges nothing. It's the right demo case because it's legible and free to show without a merchant integration. It's the wrong commercial wedge, because it's the one case where the incumbents are strictly better than what Sutra ships.

## Why now, in both directions

The protocol-timing argument is real and time-limited: AP2 v0.2 shipped in April 2026 and was donated to the FIDO Alliance, and multi-principal consent is a documented, undefended gap in it today (`spec/AP2-EXTENSION.md`). Being the working reference implementation of what a multi-principal extension needs — a shared cart hash, a commit policy language, consent binding, a crash-safe sequential commit, backstop absorption, a signed receipt, a settlement-rail declaration — is a real opportunity to influence a standard while it's still being written. That's evidence of good timing for the protocol contribution, not evidence of a market opening.

The market-timing argument cuts the other way. Visa Intelligent Commerce and Prava-style merchant-scoped agent payment credentials are themselves new in 2026, with no installed merchant base yet. Arriving early means less competition for the multi-principal niche — but it also means the rail this business depends on doesn't broadly exist yet either. Sutra's timing is downstream of a bet that isn't Sutra's to make: whether merchant-side agentic commerce credentials get adopted broadly in the next one to two years. If that doesn't happen, Sutra's addressable market stays at zero regardless of execution quality here.

## Competitive landscape

**UPI Collect is the most dangerous incumbent, and it deserves to be.** For the exact scenario this product demos — four friends splitting a Koramangala dinner bill — UPI Collect, or a plain UPI transfer after one person pays, is strictly better than Sutra's own `at_venue` rail today: real-time, near-free, working across nearly every Indian bank account, drawing from balance rather than a credit line, into an app everyone already trusts. Sutra's `at_venue` rail charges nothing at all by protocol design. Anyone who has used UPI Collect will correctly notice that, on the single question of whether money moved, Sutra's flagship demo is a regression from something already free on every Indian phone. That's a real, structural limitation of the demo scenario, not a hidden one.

Where UPI Collect doesn't reach is where Sutra's actual protocol contribution lives: no shared cart hash, no cross-principal commit policy (quorum, veto, weighted threshold), no mechanism binding N people's consent to one specific merchant checkout before it happens. It's a peer-to-peer transfer rail — using it for an actual group purchase still requires someone to become a temporary custodian, collecting from N friends before placing the order, which reintroduces the fronting-and-custody risk GMP/1 exists to remove, just compressed into minutes. It also has no commit-or-abort semantics: if three of four friends pay a collect request and the fourth backs out, the organizer is holding partial funds with nothing binding them to a merchant transaction, and reconciling that is entirely manual.

Stated plainly: for low-stakes, high-trust, single-jurisdiction, pay-a-person-back scenarios, UPI Collect is a better product than anything Sutra ships today. Sutra's case is the narrower slice where the purchase must bind to one specific merchant checkout, where group consent needs real structure beyond "has everyone paid yet," or where the transaction crosses a rail UPI doesn't touch — which is real, but smaller than "group splits a bill," and most Bangalore dinner splits aren't in it.

**Splitwise.** On the charging rail, "there is no debt between members, no ledger of who owes whom" (`spec/PROTOCOL.md` §8) is a genuine structural difference — everybody pays the merchant directly through their own capped mandate, so nobody owes anybody, because nobody borrowed anything. The honest complication: the `at_venue` rail is, in substance, a single-event ledger entry, without the feature that makes Splitwise sticky — netting balances across an ongoing group relationship. What Sutra adds there is a hash-chained, offline-verifiable record of one agreement — a different kind of trust object than a database row, but a narrower claim than "we beat Splitwise."

**Venmo/Zelle** share UPI Collect's shape for the US market — peer-transfer rails with a "charge multiple people" feature layered on top, same structural limits. Zelle settles with no reversal mechanism once sent, arguably worse for a group purchase than either UPI Collect or a card-mandate rail, since there's no dispute path if the organizer simply doesn't buy the thing.

**Point-of-sale split tender.** Shopify POS, like most modern terminals, already lets a cashier split one bill across several physical cards at the register — for a group at the counter together, this is a complete, zero-software answer today. Sutra's own POS path doesn't connect to the terminal; it prepares the split and hands it to the cashier. The honest value-add is "your shares were computed before you reached the register," not "this does something POS split tender couldn't."

**"One person just fronts it"** is the real incumbent and the hardest to beat: zero new software, zero new trust, zero friction when the group already trusts each other and the amount is small. It stops working as ticket size grows, group trust drops, or the fronting person's credit or cash flow can't absorb the float — a real but materially smaller segment than "every group purchase," and the business case has to be built on that smaller segment.

## Unit economics

No number here comes from Prava's actual pricing, which isn't published or disclosed to this team — every figure is an explicitly labeled illustrative assumption, chosen to make the arithmetic legible.

Splitting one purchase into N card-network authorizations is intrinsically more processing-fee-expensive than a single checkout, and the excess scales with group size, not purchase size. At an illustrative 2.9% + $0.30 per authorization, a $600 checkout costs $17.70 to process; split four ways it costs $18.60 — an extra $0.90, almost entirely the three extra fixed per-transaction fees. Run the same group size against a $40 purchase and the excess is again $0.90 — but as a fraction of GMV that's 0.15% on the $600 order versus 2.25% on the $40 order, fifteen times larger proportionally on the small order. Sutra should not chase small, frequent splits on unit economics grounds alone.

At an illustrative 1.5%-of-GMV take rate, the $600 order clears $9.00 against that $0.90 tax — a workable margin, though whether Sutra or the merchant actually bears the $0.90 depends on a commercial agreement with Prava that doesn't exist yet. The $40 order clears $0.60, less than the fragmentation tax alone: structurally loss-making before support or fraud costs, and a second independent confirmation the business only works at higher ticket sizes. As an illustrative, explicitly-not-a-forecast figure: 5,000 completed group orders a month at $400 average GMV and a 1.5% take rate is $2M of monthly GMV and $30,000 of gross revenue — a number chosen only to make the arithmetic concrete, with no data behind it, and reaching it requires solving the cold-start problem below.

There is no measured CAC, because there are no customers. A B2B motion selling group-checkout to a handful of ticketing or travel platforms carries a sales-cycle cost plausibly in the low thousands per relationship, amortized over whatever volume that merchant brings. A consumer viral loop — every approval link sent to a not-yet-user — has a plausible near-zero marginal CAC if invitees go on to organize their own groups, the same mechanic that grew Venmo; whether that loop converts invitees into repeat organizers versus one-time approvers is completely unproven, and the product doesn't currently require account creation to approve a share at all.

## Who pays Sutra

Three models, not equally credible near-term. A **merchant take rate** on completed group GMV is the natural long-run model with the clearest precedent in payment facilitation and BNPL, but it requires exactly the thing this business doesn't have yet: a merchant relationship. A **B2B licensing or integration fee** sold to platforms is the most credible near-term model, because it turns merchant cold-start into a sales motion instead of a consumer-acquisition chicken-and-egg problem — revenue exists from the first signed platform. A **consumer coordination fee** charged to the organizer is the weakest: it introduces a new ask at exactly the moment of a payment decision, competing against a free default that most groups will take unless the merchant-side value (guaranteed collection, no chargeback exposure) is compelling enough that the merchant pays instead. The recommended sequencing: sell integration to platforms first, evolve toward a blended take rate once volume exists, treat a consumer fee as a later supplement at most.

## The two-sided cold start

Consumers have no reason to adopt Sutra until merchants they want to buy from support it; merchants have no reason to integrate until there's consumer volume to justify the engineering cost of stable quoting, N real captures, reconciliation, and refund/fulfilment handling. The credible path is the standard playbook for this shape of cold start: concentrate on supply first. Pick one or a few anchor merchants in the ticketing wedge — platforms that already serve large numbers of consumer groups through a single integration point — and build the dedicated adapter for that one relationship, rather than trying to be a generic merchant-agnostic product from day one. That converts "get every merchant to adopt an unproven protocol" into "close one sales relationship and build one integration," at the cost of narrowing the product to whatever that merchant's category needs.

## What would have to be true, and what would kill it

A production merchant adapter processing real captures, not test-mode ones. A proven wedge vertical with organizer pain validated by interviews or a signed pilot, not assumption. A revenue model validated against at least one paying merchant, rather than the illustrative arithmetic above. A ticket-size mix concentrated at the higher end, where the fragmentation tax stays a small fraction of GMV. Evidence the invite loop converts approvers into repeat organizers, not just one-time acceptors. A resolved dispute-and-refund procedure (below), because "ask the merchant or open a chargeback" is not an answer a payments business can operate at scale on. And legal validation, jurisdiction by jurisdiction, of the "not a money transmitter" posture that `spec/PROTOCOL.md` §8 and `spec/AP2-EXTENSION.md` currently argue only as an engineering property — a sound description of the architecture, not a compliance opinion, and treating it as one before a lawyer says so would be exactly the kind of overclaim this project otherwise avoids.

What kills it: merchant non-adoption, full stop. An incumbent (UPI, Venmo, Zelle) bolting a merchant-checkout-binding feature onto its existing peer-transfer rail — a smaller lift for them, given they already have distribution and regulatory relationships, than building multi-principal commit policies from scratch is for Sutra. Chargeback and dispute costs at scale exceeding whatever take rate is charged — the classic failure mode of marketplace-adjacent payments businesses, and discussed concretely below because it is not hypothetical here. Regulatory reclassification as a money transmitter if pooling ever creeps in under growth pressure — the single most common bad decision growth-stage fintechs make. And, more mundanely, the high-ticket, infrequent-purchase niche this document argues is the only sound wedge turning out to be real but simply too small to be a venture-scale business on its own.

## The chargeback and refund story

Most of the ways a group purchase could go wrong are caught before any card is charged: policy evaluation locks the approver set before commit preparation begins, a shortfall is covered by an armed backstop or a requote before the charging saga starts, a `straggler_policy` (`retry_once`, `drop_and_continue`, `halt_partial`) governs a failure during the sequential commit, and an unknown charge result is never treated as a failure — the engine asks Prava for the idempotency reference before deciding anything, so a retry never becomes a double charge. All of this is tested, not merely asserted, by `engine/test/crash-double-charge.test.ts`.

Where the well-handled mechanics stop: card four declining after cards one through three have already captured is a genuine card-network failure striking during the sequential charge loop itself, after the first captures already landed for real. `straggler_policy` governs the reaction; it can't prevent the moment, because the failure is the card network, not a coordination bug. What the three already-charged people get today is a `partial` group status and a signed receipt naming exactly what each was charged — real, verifiable evidence, but not a remedy. **Sutra does not initiate a refund, contact the merchant, or file anything on the charged members' behalf.** A post-capture reversal raises `RefundNotSupportedError`, whose message carries the only real remedy: a merchant-initiated refund against the recorded transaction id, or a cardholder chargeback with their own bank — a limit of the underlying Prava charge primitive itself (a real capture, not a delayed authorization that could simply be released), not something specific to the NANDA plugin. There is no automated remedy in the current build.

What a real product needs to add, named as roadmap and not overstated as built: auto-drafting a merchant refund request from data that already exists; making `halt_partial` the default above some ticket-size threshold, since it minimizes blast radius even though it produces more `partial` outcomes; and investigating whether Prava could support authorize-then-delayed-capture across all N holds simultaneously, so a late decline releases an authorization instead of requiring a refund of money already settled.

Chargebacks carry real per-dispute fees, commonly cited industry-wide as roughly $15–25 (illustrative, not Prava-specific). Past a dispute rate of roughly 1% of volume, a business can have its payment processing put at risk entirely, independent of product quality — a real, currently uncosted operational liability that any credible take rate has to price in from the first transaction.

## In one paragraph

Zero users, no signed merchant, no validated pricing, and one genuinely unresolved operational gap in how three charged people get their money back when a fourth person's card declines mid-commit. Also: a real, narrow wedge in high-ticket, infrequent group purchases where the unit economics actually work; a specific argument for why small, frequent splits do not and should not be chased; an honest concession that UPI Collect beats this product outright on the exact scenario it demos; and a genuine, currently timely protocol contribution whose entire commercial value depends on a merchant-adoption bet that nobody — not this team, not the market yet — has actually made.
